Trump, the “Madman” Method, and Moral Regression

What may appear in the business world as a harsh but tolerated tactic can, in international affairs, become a threat to the very restraint on which modern civilization rests.

Amnon Zussman | April 8, 2026

Perhaps Trump’s style can only be fully understood if we begin with the world from which he came. In business, an aggressive, threatening, and at times “dirty” method can produce quick gains. It may generate short-term profit for him, while causing competitors various financial losses — and in most cases, that is where the matter ends.

The capitalist world has never entirely disqualified such methods. As long as they remain legal, the center of gravity stays on the bottom line. The first question is not always whether a method is elegant, moral, or worthy, but whether it works and whether it can be defended legally. That is why a forceful, intimidating, and uninhibited figure can still be seen there as highly effective.

The problem begins when that pattern moves from business into international diplomacy. Even in statecraft, there is not always a clear law against such methods. But there is something else: a kind of political morality that evolved over history, a normative layer formed after the broader development of human morality itself. It is an imperfect sphere of restraint, proportion, responsibility, and language meant to prevent raw power from swallowing everything.

If one looks at Trump’s conduct in a specific moment of crisis, his method may indeed appear effective. He threatens without proportion, creates the sense that nothing limits him, and signals to his rivals that this is not the ordinary game of judgment and restraint. Faced with such a figure, armed with enormous power, even a determined adversary may hesitate.

That may, in fact, be the core of the method: not real madness, but a calculated performance of madness. If the other side believes it is dealing with someone fundamentally unpredictable, it may prefer retreat, compromise, or a ceasefire. In the test of immediate results, this can sometimes seem to work.

But the important question is not only whether the method succeeds in one case. The real question is what happens to a world in which such a method becomes a model to imitate. At that point, this is no longer merely a matter of one leader’s style, but a challenge to the moral direction of humanity itself.

For most of history, unrestrained power was not seen as a flaw but as a virtue. Rulers were measured by their ability to instill fear, destroy, and deter. Only gradually — at the cost of wars, religions, revolutions, and catastrophes — did the idea emerge that power, too, must have limits: law, diplomacy, political responsibility, human rights, and the laws of war.

This may be civilization’s greatest achievement: not that human beings ceased to be violent, but that they learned to see unrestrained violence as a failure, not an accomplishment. The great historical effort was to domesticate power — not to abolish it, but to restrain it.

Trump’s method threatens precisely that achievement. It restores to the center stage the figure of the leader whose power rests on the fact that no one knows how far he is willing to go. If this becomes an accepted language, every crisis may turn into a contest of intimidation, and every leader will want to appear as someone without brakes.

The world will not return backward with spears and swords. It will move backward with missiles, cyberwarfare, precision weapons, and digital propaganda. Barbarism will not return in ancient dress, but in technological form.

That is why, even if the “madman” method can sometimes produce a tactical result, it must not be confused with genuine statesmanship. It may impose a temporary halt, but it struggles to build stable order, long-term trust, or lasting peace. The problem with Trump is not only Trump himself — it is the precedent he leaves behind.

We will not return to the barbarians of the first millennium — we will return to the barbarism of the third.
